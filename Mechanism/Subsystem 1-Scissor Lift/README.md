## Bearings in the scissor lift

The scissor lift raises and lowers the whole chair. Two kinds of bearings keep the hand crank light and the motion smooth: **track rollers** at the sliding ends of the scissor arms, and a **thrust bearing block** on the lead screw.

### 1. Track rollers (item 10): 4 rollers, 8 bearings
A scissor lift has one fixed end and one sliding end. As the chair goes up, the sliding ends of the X-shaped arms move along the frame, the top end along the top frame and the bottom end along the base.

- Each sliding cross-shaft (5 at the top, 8 at the bottom) has a short Ø15 steel stub at each end.
- On each stub sits a **track roller** made of **2 × 6202-2RS ball bearings** side by side (15 mm bore × 35 mm outside × 11 mm wide each), giving a roller Ø35 × 22 mm.
- The outer rings of the bearings roll along steel tracks: in the base frame (1) for shaft 8, and in the top frame (11) for shaft 5.
- 4 rollers in total = 8 bearings. "2RS" means rubber seals on both sides: no oiling needed, and dust stays out.

### 2. Thrust bearing block (item 14): lead screw support
The crank (15) turns a lead screw (12, Tr20×4). The screw pulls a bronze nut (13), the nut pulls the top sliding shaft, and the arms stand up. While doing this, the screw is pulled along its own length with a force of several kilonewtons, up to about 11 kN in the design load case.

- At the crank end, the screw runs through a steel block holding two bearings:
  - **51104 thrust bearing** (20 × 35 × 10): takes the **axial pull** along the screw.
  - **6004 deep-groove ball bearing** (20 × 42 × 12): holds the screw **centred** and takes the side (radial) load from the crank.


**Note: the screw still can't run back by itself.** The bearings only reduce friction at the block. The self-locking comes from the screw thread itself: its 4.0° thread angle is below the 7.1° friction angle. So the bearings make cranking easier without making the lift slip. Let go of the crank and the chair stays where it is.
